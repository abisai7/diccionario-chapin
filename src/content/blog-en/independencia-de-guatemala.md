## The day that changed Central America

Every **September 15**, chapines paint the streets blue and white to remember a date that shaped the history of all Central America. In 1821, Guatemala, then the capital of a kingdom that also included El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Chiapas, proclaimed its independence from Spain. On that day, the document we know today as the **Act of Independence** was signed in the city's central plaza.

## Three centuries of the Captaincy General

After the arrival of the Spanish in the 16th century, the territory was organized as part of the **Kingdom of Guatemala**, also known as the Captaincy General of Guatemala. For a long time its capital was **Antigua Guatemala**, which after the 1773 earthquakes was replaced by **Nueva Guatemala de la Asunción**, the city we know today.

For nearly three hundred years, the kingdom functioned under Spanish crown rule. But in the early 19th century, the winds of independence swept across the Americas: Mexico with the **Plan of Iguala** of Agustín de Iturbide, and the south with the campaigns of **Simón Bolívar** and **José de San Martín**. Guatemala could not stay on the sidelines.

## The night of September 14

On **September 14, 1821**, the superior political chief **Gabino Gaínza** summoned the authorities and notable citizens of the kingdom to the **Palace of the Captain Generals**. The news spread through the city, and people began to gather in the plaza to learn the kingdom's fate.

According to accounts of the time, it was then that **María Dolores Bedoya**, remembered today as one of the heroines of independence, walked through the streets encouraging the people, handing out flowers and blue and white ribbons, and spreading her enthusiasm to everyone. The notables deliberated all night while the crowd waited outside.

## September 15, 1821

On the morning of September 15, the **Act of Independence**, drafted by **José Cecilio del Valle**, was read and signed. The document proclaimed the separation of the Kingdom of Guatemala from the Spanish monarchy and invited all provinces of the former kingdom to send deputies to a general congress to decide their form of government.

The news spread throughout the territory. It is said that **the bells rang out** and the plaza burst with joy; popular legend remembers three young men, **Pepín, Mariano, and Máximo**, who in different accounts ran through the streets carrying the news from house to house.

## The faces of independence

Guatemalan independence had no single protagonist. Among the most remembered names are:

- **José Cecilio del Valle**, who drafted the act and, despite his reservations about the speed of the process, left his mark as one of the most brilliant thinkers of his time.
- **Pedro Molina** and **José Francisco Barrundia**, liberal supporters of independence who advanced the cause through the written press and city council debates.
- **María Dolores Bedoya**, whose work in the plaza made her a symbol of the people's participation in the 1821 feat.
- **Basilio Porras, José Mariano de Castro, Rafael de Monteros**, and other signers of the act, whose signatures were sealed into national memory.

## The road after independence

The independence of 1821 was the beginning, not the end, of a long journey. Shortly after, Guatemala briefly joined the **First Mexican Empire** under Iturbide (1822-1823) and then became part of the **United Provinces of Central America**, a federation that eventually dissolved around 1840. From then on, Guatemala consolidated itself as an independent republic, but September 15 remained the shared symbol of the birth of the Central American nations.

## The celebration today: torches, lanterns, and parades

Today, September 15 is one of the most beloved celebrations for chapines. Days before, **the torch relays of independence** travel across the country and Central America, lighting the path of the towns. On the night of September 14, thousands of Guatemalans take to the streets with **handmade paper lanterns**.

And on the morning of September 15, the student parade with its **school marching bands** fills the avenues with blue and white. The sky-blue flag with the quetzal and the pinwheels are waved, the **ceiba**, our national tree, is raised, and the **National Anthem** is sung at the top of everyone's lungs while the marimba thunders in every corner.

> _"September 15 is not just a date: it is the day when all chapines feel, more than ever, Central American at heart."_
