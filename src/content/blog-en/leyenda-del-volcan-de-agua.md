## A mountain with history

The **Volcán de Agua** is one of Guatemala's most iconic volcanoes. Standing 3,716 meters above sea level, it rises majestically in the department of Sacatepéquez, guarding Antigua Guatemala. Unlike its neighbors, Volcán de Fuego and Acatenango, the Volcán de Agua is an **extinct** volcano, and the Kaqchikel people say a beautiful lagoon formed in its crater.

## Princess Ixcucuyá

Legend has it that the Kaqchikel king **Ahij Xic** ruled the highland towns and had a daughter of extraordinary beauty, Princess **Ixcucuyá**, daughter of the Moon. Her fame spread through all the kingdoms, and princes and noble warriors came from distant lands to ask for her hand in marriage.

Among all the suitors, one stood out: **the Prince of Fires**, son of the Volcán de Fuego. Brave and fiery, he captured the admiration of the court. The king decided to give the princess to the Prince of Fires — but **Ixcucuyá's heart belonged to another**: a humble farmer from the village whom she met in secret.

## Love and conflict

When the princess rejected the powerful son of the Volcán de Fuego, he felt humiliated and his fury overflowed. From his volcano he hurled **rivers of lava, stones, and ash** over the highlands, threatening to destroy the entire kingdom.

Desperate to save her people, Princess Ixcucuyá climbed the neighboring mountain and asked her mother, the Moon, to turn her into water that could quench the fire. **The princess's water cascaded down the mountain**, smothering the flames and saving the communities in the valleys.

## The origin of the lagoon

At the summit of the mountain a crater remained, forming a lagoon. The Kaqchikel people eventually called it the **lagoon of the Volcán de Agua**, saying its waters are the princess's tears, still remembering her beloved farmer in the valley below.

Some versions say the farmer, watching his beloved's transformation, became a weeping willow forever at the lagoon's shore. Others say it was the king who ordered the lagoon built as an offering. Like every legend, each region tells its own version — but **they all agree on the princess's sacrifice and love** for her people.

## The mountain today

Today the Volcán de Agua is also known as **Hunahpú**, in the Kaqchikel Mayan language, and remains a symbol of Guatemalan identity. Every year, climbers and pilgrims ascend to its summit to admire the crater lagoon and the spectacular panorama of the Valley of Guatemala.

Its silhouette still watches over Antigua Guatemala, reminding us that behind every Guatemalan mountain lies **a story waiting to be discovered**.
